Ocean acidification has accelerated significantly since the Industrial Revolution, driven primarily by atmospheric carbon dioxide absorption. The pH of seawater has decreased by 0.1 units since pre-industrial times, representing a 30 percent increase in acidity. This change threatens shellfish larvae, coral reefs, and pteropods—small marine organisms that form the base of many oceanic food webs. The Great Barrier Reef has experienced four mass bleaching events in the past eight years, with 2022 and 2023 seeing unprecedented simultaneous bleaching across all regions. Marine biologists estimate that if current warming trends continue, coral reefs could cease to exist as functioning ecosystems by 2050. Carbon removal technologies, including direct air capture and ocean alkalinity enhancement, could theoretically remove 10 gigatons of CO2 annually by 2050. However, these technologies currently cost between 200 and 1,000 dollars per ton of CO2 removed, making them economically unfeasible at scale without substantial subsidy. Meanwhile, renewable energy transition has progressed faster than most predictions: wind and solar now represent approximately 12 percent of global electricity generation, up from 2 percent in 2010. Scientists emphasize that limiting ocean warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius requires reducing global emissions by 45 percent by 2030 compared to 2010 levels.
